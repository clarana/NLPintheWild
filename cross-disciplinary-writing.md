# Cross-disciplinary writing exercise

**Due Tuesday, November 17 at 11:59pm ET.**

By now, you have a sense of your project's goals and potential outcomes. Today, each member of your project group will write a (mock) abstract for your project as it would appear in a venue from their own field. With your group, you articulate and learn about disciplinary norms, as well as relate disciplinary writing conventions to [community](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_community) norms and practices-- consider the intended audience, reviewing and publishing practices, and what counts as a contribution.

## Learning objectives

1. Understand and describe community norms and practices across multiple disciplines, and how these norms and practices can directly or indirectly influence the work that gets published
2. Compare and contrast norms across disciplines and communities
3. Identify conventions you follow consciously or instinctively in your own writing, and consider their suitability and utility in at least one setting that you are less familiar with.

## Instructions for writing your abstract

We are intentionally not prescribing a specific word count, since length is itself one of the conventions that differs between communities. Write the abstract as you would for a venue or audience you know, following whatever length and formatting rules you are aware of (for example, a 150-word limit, or required section headings). If you are not sure, aim for a single paragraph of at most ~300 words or so. **Since your project is not finished, feel free to make up plausible placeholder results as needed.**  As you write, please keep a short list of any conventions you are following on purpose, such as length limits, required sections, whether you use "we" or "I", terminology that may hold a different meaning in other contexts, etc. Please also describe any *personal* (or non-general) stylistic or editorial tendencies you find yourself writing with. You will use this list when comparing abstracts with your group. (Additionally, after comparison, you will describe any conventions you did not necessarily realize you were following)

Feel free to write more informally for most of this assignment i.e. the discussion and reflection questions preceding and following the abstract writing itself. However, please take the time to refine your abstract more carefully than we normally ask for other writing you have done for this class so far. We are not necessarily looking for spelling/grammar that aligns perfectly with the conventions of Standard American English (and in fact, your community might *not* use Standard American English), *but* we ask that you write **intentionally** i.e. that you make conscious decisions about format, length, voice, and style.

Note: This activity was designed with academic research in mind, so the examples and the word "abstract" come from research publishing. If you have research experience, we assume you will complete this activity using your familiarity with a particular academic discipline's writing and publishing practices. If you do not have academic research experience, or you feel much more familiar with a *non-research* disciplinary writing practice, you are free to ground your activity participation in any discipline whose (writing) norms you are familiar with. Valid and welcomed examples of types of writing include technical documentation for software engineers, art critiques, policy documents, long-form fiction (for traditional publishing or even a D&D campaign!), etc. Basically, anything where you can describe the practices, norms, and explicit and implicit expectations around written communication, perhaps after a little poking around to re-orient yourself. If "abstracts" are not normal in your community, write your community's closest equivalent (e.g. an executive summary, a README overview, a pitch).


## Steps

1. [individual] Name a specific set of research venues or a type of writing that you are familiar with. Describe in 3-5 sentences some of the characteristics of the community and the writing conventions of your publication type.
2. [individual] Discuss in 2-3 sentences: Did you always find these conventions intuitive to follow? Do you feel that any in particular could be unintuitive to a newcomer? What function do these conventions serve? Do any of these conventions feel outdated?
3. [individual] Write a draft of your abstract: see above for detailed instructions. Remember to include not only your abstract but also a list of conventions you are consciously following as you are writing it. Spend no more than 15 minutes on this initial step.
4. [group] With your group, discuss your answers for 1-2. You may wish to show each other examples of abstracts from your respective fields to ground your discussion. Please write 3-7 sentences describing your observations and discussion. Make sure to describe the fields, communities, and publication types represented and examples shown if any-- beyond that, describe similarities, differences, impressions, confusions, amusements, etc. If there are multiple group members writing from the same field, did they have similar answers?
5. [group] (Paste an abstract + list of conventions written by a group member who wrote from a different field compared to you for our reference, either their initial draft or their refined version.) Swap abstracts and lists of conventions (consciously) followed with a group member who wrote from a different field. Write 3-7 sentences on your observations and discussion. Guiding questions: Similarities and differences? Does anything feel like jargon? Do you notice anything about their writing that they didn't describe in their list? Did *they* notice things about your writing that weren't on your list? Are these things disciplinary conventions that were followed subconsciously, individual stylistic tendencies, or something else? Do you find the abstracts equally comprehensible?
6. [individual] Refine your abstract. All types of revisions are valid-- you may make local changes, fix spelling, reorganize the ideas in your abstract, etc. A good general goal, however, would be to improve clarity to people outside your field while staying roughly within your own community's disciplinary norms. (If you feel improving clarity to people *in general* is impractical (valid answer), describe briefly why! You may instead consider focusing on a "scoped" audience of e.g. graduate students in 11-811, or just your teammates). Please keep both your draft and your "final" version! If you are comfortable, turn in both (your "changelog" describing any differences between your versions may be less detailed in this case). Either way, please describe what revisions you are making and towards what goals. Optional but recommended: ask someone from your intended audience to read your abstract and comment on its clarity!
7. Provide a statement describing collaboration and resource use (including any AI use-- see below)


## Collaboration and allowed resources

Importantly, for this assignment, for questions marked [individual], we ask that you think through and complete them on your own first prior to any discussion with your group members (in contrast, previous assignments welcomed collaboration as long as you wrote your own answers before submission). This is not to discourage discussion and collaboration (which is an important part of this assignment) but to encourage students to compare *independently* observed practices and norms. As before, [group] questions expect collaboration and may contain submitted language similar or identical to other group members'.

By default, we ask all students to **refrain from AI use** for all questions, other than possibly transcription technology (speech-to-text) as part of your process. Answers should be written by human students! You may use the internet for general resources to understand subject material. For questions 3 and 6, please also refine your abstract on your own. As described above, this means conscious decisions about format, length, voice, and style, not necessarily polished grammar. Talk to us if you would like to request an exception!
